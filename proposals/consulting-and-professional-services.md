# Proposal: Consulting and Professional Services as a Later-Stage Reform

## Status

MVP proposal note.

## Summary

Consulting and professional services are a major long-term target for the Paid in Australia project because they involve substantial government spending, skilled labour, recurring use of panels, and high potential for corporate overhead and profit extraction.

This sector should not be treated as the first pilot. It is strategically important, but legally, administratively, and politically more complex than early-adoption sectors such as childcare, education-related services, NDIS and disability services, or waste removal.

## Why consulting matters

Government consulting often relies on highly skilled workers who could, in some cases, deliver similar services through smaller Public-Benefit Suppliers, mutuals, cooperatives, university-linked entities, or worker-led not-for-distribution firms.

The policy question is whether public agencies could receive equivalent or better expertise with lower corporate overheads, stronger transparency, clearer intellectual property rules, better public-sector capability transfer, and stronger reinvestment obligations.

## Why this should be later-stage

Consulting reform should follow earlier pilots because:

1. consulting contracts vary widely in purpose, risk, confidentiality, and complexity
2. large consultancies are deeply embedded in panel systems
3. some consulting work involves specialised capacity that may not be quickly replaced
4. procurement rules and conflict-of-interest rules are especially important
5. data on contract scope, subcontracting, profit margin, and staff cost is often limited
6. reforms must avoid simply shifting expenditure from one large supplier category to another

## Initial research tasks

1. Build a dataset of Commonwealth consulting contracts using AusTender.
2. Extend the dataset to state, territory, and local procurement portals where data is available.
3. Identify the largest consulting and professional services suppliers by contract value, number of contracts, agencies served, and panel use.
4. Compare contract values with public financial statements, partner remuneration where available, corporate tax transparency data, and ownership structures.
5. Identify service categories that could plausibly be delivered by Public-Benefit Suppliers.
6. Identify functions that should instead be rebuilt inside the public service.
7. Identify functions that require temporary specialist external input.

## Possible reform pathway

The long-term pathway may include:

- disclosure of day rates, subcontracting, and senior staff allocation
- mandatory public-sector capability transfer plans
- limits on repeated outsourcing of core government work
- preference for Public-Benefit Suppliers in suitable professional service categories
- stronger conflict-of-interest disclosure
- panel refresh requirements
- open data on quotes sought and value-for-money assessment
- public reporting of Australian ownership, tax paid, and profit distribution

## Open questions

- Which consulting categories are most suitable for Public-Benefit Supplier delivery?
- Which consulting categories should be rebuilt as public-sector capacity instead?
- Which suppliers receive the largest public contracts across Commonwealth, state, territory, and local levels?
- How much tax do those suppliers pay in Australia?
- What proportion of public contract value becomes wages, domestic reinvestment, profit, offshore payments, or partner distributions?
