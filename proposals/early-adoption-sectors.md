# Proposal: Early Adoption Sectors for Public-Benefit Supplier Status

## Status

MVP proposal note. This document replaces earlier single-sector example notes.

## Summary

The first implementation phase should test Public-Benefit Supplier status in sectors where government funding already creates or sustains a market, where public value is central, and where existing regulation can reduce implementation risk.

Childcare, education, NDIS and disability services, and waste removal are proposed as candidate early-adoption sectors. These are not intended to be the only sectors. They are starting points for consultation, evidence gathering, and public submissions.

The childcare example is included because Australia already has a substantial not-for-profit and public-benefit tradition in early childhood education and care, although those providers do not operate under the proposed Public-Benefit Supplier status. This makes childcare useful as a practical reference point, not as a claim that the current system already solves the problem.

## Why a combined early-adoption proposal

The project should not appear to be about one sector only. The policy is a general supplier-status and procurement reform. Early sectors should be selected because they are practical places to test the mechanism.

A combined proposal allows contributors to compare candidate sectors against common criteria:

1. scale of public funding
2. quality and safety sensitivity
3. existing regulation
4. workforce stability problems
5. exposure to profit extraction or ownership leakage
6. feasibility of forming new Public-Benefit Suppliers
7. risk of service disruption
8. availability of public data
9. suitability for local, state, territory, or federal pilots
10. community support and political feasibility

## Candidate early sectors

### Early childhood education and care

Early childhood education and care is a useful starting point because government subsidy, quality regulation, workforce issues, parental affordability, and public benefit are already central to the sector. The sector already includes public, community, and not-for-profit providers, making it a practical reference point for testing whether stronger reinvestment and workforce obligations could improve public value.

The proposal should examine whether Public-Benefit Supplier status could apply to new centres, expanded places, underserved areas, or government-funded growth programs before considering broader sector-wide rules.

### Education-related services

Education-related services may include tutoring programs, school support services, vocational training support, student wellbeing services, technology support, facilities services, and other publicly funded education-adjacent contracts. This sector is suitable for review because education is a public-good domain, but many support services are delivered through external providers.

The early question is not whether all education services should shift to Public-Benefit Suppliers. The question is which externally procured services are most suitable for pilot procurement preferences.

### NDIS and disability services

NDIS and disability services are candidate sectors because the market is publicly funded, quality and safety sensitive, and already regulated. Public-Benefit Supplier status could be tested first in selected service types where continuity, workforce stability, participant trust, and reinvestment obligations are especially important.

This proposal should be careful not to assume that every NDIS service can be transitioned quickly. Thin markets, regional access, participant choice, and provider viability must be central to the design.

### Waste removal and local government services

Waste removal is a strong candidate because local government procurement often involves long-term contracts, place-based delivery, essential service continuity, and recurring public expenditure. Public-Benefit Supplier pilots could test whether local reinvestment, secure employment, transparent subcontracting, and capped executive overheads improve value in recurring municipal services.

Waste removal may also allow early pilots at council level before state or federal adoption.

## Submissions requested

Contributors are invited to propose additional early-adoption sectors. A submission should address:

- why the sector is suitable for early adoption
- which level of government is responsible for most spending
- whether existing regulation can support implementation
- current provider types and market concentration
- public spending scale
- workforce structure
- risks of disruption
- available data sources
- likely legal constraints
- whether a pilot should use preference, mandate, grant condition, or transparency rules

## Initial implementation model

Early adoption should proceed in stages:

1. collect sector submissions
2. map public spending and existing providers
3. identify legal and procurement constraints
4. design voluntary Public-Benefit Supplier registration
5. test procurement scoring preference in selected contracts
6. evaluate quality, cost, wages, provider stability, and public value retained
7. publish results before broader rollout

## Open questions

- Which sectors should be first, and why?
- Should early pilots occur through federal programs, state programs, local councils, or all three?
- Should pilots apply only to new contracts or also to renewals?
- How should service users be represented in sector selection?
- How can the policy avoid disrupting small local providers?
- What data should suppliers be required to disclose before any preference is applied?
