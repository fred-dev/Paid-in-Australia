# Core Principles

## 1. Public money should create public value

Government spending should be assessed not only by the immediate purchase price, but by its wider effect on service quality, workforce security, domestic capacity, public trust, and long term Australian benefit.

## 2. Competition can operate within public-benefit rules

The proposal does not require government ownership of suppliers. It allows independent organisations to compete, innovate, specialise, and improve services. The difference is that suppliers receiving public money would operate under stronger public-benefit obligations.

## 3. Not-for-profit does not mean charity

Paid in Australia proposes a new Public-Benefit Supplier status. This status should be distinct from charity registration. It should be available to organisations that serve public purposes through government funded work but do not necessarily meet the legal test for charitable purpose.

## 4. Surplus should be reinvested, not extracted

A Public-Benefit Supplier may generate a surplus. The key restriction is that surplus should not be privately distributed. It should be reinvested into approved purposes, including service quality, lower prices, workforce capability, equipment, regional access, reserves, or long term service resilience.

## 5. Worker security is part of public value

Government funded markets should not rely on insecure employment as a hidden subsidy. Public-Benefit Suppliers should provide secure employment, fair wage structures, and transparent remuneration practices.

## 6. Australian circulation matters

Where public money flows to for-profit suppliers, policy should seek to keep a greater share of resulting profit connected to Australians. Australian superannuation is one possible pathway, but it must be designed consistently with trustee duties and member interests.

## 7. Reform should be staged

The policy should begin with transparency, pilots, procurement scoring, model structures, and evidence gathering before moving to hard mandates.

## 8. Legal constraints must be treated seriously

Procurement obligations, superannuation trustee duties, competition law, corporations law, constitutional issues, and trade commitments must shape implementation.
