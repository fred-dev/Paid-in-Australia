# Policy Stream A: Public-Benefit Suppliers

## Purpose

This policy stream creates a new regulated supplier status for government funded markets. The status is called Public-Benefit Supplier.

The aim is to allow capable organisations to compete for public work while accepting stronger obligations than ordinary for-profit suppliers.

## Why a new status is needed

Existing charity law is not the right vehicle for the full proposal. Many suppliers that deliver public value will not have a charitable purpose. Maintenance providers, engineering consultancies, disability service providers, childcare operators, transport providers, facilities managers, software providers, and local infrastructure contractors may all perform publicly valuable work without being charities.

The Public-Benefit Supplier status should therefore be separate from charity registration. It should be designed for government funded markets rather than philanthropic or charitable activity.

## Possible legal form

A Public-Benefit Supplier could be established through one or more legal forms, including:

- a company limited by guarantee
- a purpose specific statutory public-benefit corporation
- a cooperative or mutual structure
- a public company with constitutional restrictions on distribution
- another approved not-for-distribution structure

The key issue is not the label of the entity, but the enforceable public-benefit obligations attached to it.

## Core obligations

A Public-Benefit Supplier should be required to:

1. reinvest all surplus into approved public-benefit purposes
2. prohibit private distribution of profit or surplus
3. publish transparent accounts
4. disclose related party transactions
5. comply with executive remuneration limits
6. comply with a highest to lowest pay ratio
7. limit subcontracting leakage
8. provide secure employment for ordinary ongoing work
9. maintain appropriate insurance and professional standards
10. meet sector specific quality and safety standards
11. report annually on public-benefit outcomes
12. accept enforcement action for serious or repeated non-compliance

## Remuneration controls

The MVP proposes a maximum individual remuneration cap of AUD 900,000 per year. This should include salary, bonuses, allowances, contractor payments, related entity payments, and other benefits.

The MVP also proposes a maximum pay ratio between the highest and lowest paid workers. The exact ratio should be tested through sector modelling.

The aim is not to suppress skilled wages. The aim is to prevent public money from supporting excessive executive extraction while allowing skilled workers to earn strong salaries in lean public-benefit organisations.

## Employment standards

Public-Benefit Suppliers should not rely on casual employment for ordinary ongoing work.

The default should be permanent full time or permanent part time employment. Exceptions should be limited, defined, and reported.

## Subcontracting controls

A Public-Benefit Supplier should not be able to avoid its obligations by subcontracting most of the work to ordinary for-profit entities.

The policy should include a subcontracting cap, with exemptions for genuinely specialised, temporary, emergency, regional, or approved transitional work.

## Formation pathway

The reform should include a subsidised formation pathway for skilled workers and local operators who want to leave large corporate suppliers and create Public-Benefit Suppliers.

Support may include:

- model constitutions
- registration assistance
- legal templates
- insurance support
- startup loans
- payroll and compliance support
- procurement onboarding
- shared back office services
- technical assistance
- mentoring
- transition contracts

## Procurement preference

Government procurement should gradually preference Public-Benefit Suppliers in suitable sectors.

The first stage should use scoring preferences and pilots rather than immediate exclusion of other suppliers.

Later stages may create mandatory preference, reserved procurement categories, or sector specific supplier eligibility rules where legal and practical evidence supports this.

## Enforcement

The enforcement model should be graduated.

Possible responses include:

- corrective action notices
- enforceable undertakings
- public reporting of non-compliance
- repayment of misused funds
- civil penalties
- director or officer disqualification
- loss of Public-Benefit Supplier status
- exclusion from future procurement
- referral to existing criminal law processes where conduct may already constitute fraud, false accounting, wage theft, bribery, corruption, or deliberate evasion

The policy does not use imprisonment as a standard regulatory penalty.
