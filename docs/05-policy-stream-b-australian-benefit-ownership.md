# Policy Stream B: Australian-Benefit Ownership

## Purpose

This policy stream addresses for-profit suppliers in government funded markets.

Where public money flows to for-profit companies, policy should seek to keep a greater share of resulting profits, dividends, capital gains, and long term productive value connected to Australians.

## Starting point

The original policy idea proposed that large for-profit government suppliers should have majority ownership by Australian superannuation providers.

This remains a core direction for analysis, but the MVP treats it as a staged and legally complex pathway rather than an immediate hard rule.

## Why caution is needed

Superannuation trustees are required to act in the interests of their members and to maintain funds for retirement purposes. Procurement rules and trade commitments may also limit direct discrimination based on ownership nationality or investor class.

For that reason, the MVP begins with mechanisms that are more legally plausible and easier to test.

## Stage 1: Transparency

Require large government suppliers to disclose:

- beneficial ownership
- ultimate parent entities
- country of ownership and control
- dividend flows
- related party payments
- offshore payments
- executive remuneration bands
- subcontracting ratios
- Australian superannuation ownership
- worker ownership
- mutual or cooperative ownership
- tax transparency indicators

## Stage 2: Procurement scoring

Where lawful, procurement scoring should recognise Australian-benefit ownership structures that demonstrably retain value in Australia.

This may include:

- substantial Australian superannuation ownership
- worker ownership
- mutual ownership
- community ownership
- sovereign backed co-investment
- domestic reinvestment commitments
- transparent limits on offshore extraction

## Stage 3: Incentives

Government may create incentives for Australian-benefit ownership, including:

- concessional finance
- co-investment vehicles
- transition loans
- risk sharing instruments
- standardised investment vehicles for superannuation participation
- procurement readiness support
- pooled vehicles to prevent concentration of control

## Stage 4: Sector specific thresholds

After legal and economic review, some sectors may justify stronger thresholds for Australian-benefit ownership.

The original proposed threshold is that at least 60 percent of ownership in relevant for-profit suppliers should be held by Australian superannuation providers, potentially distributed across multiple funds to avoid control by one fund.

This threshold should be treated as a modelling and legal review target, not as an immediate MVP rule.

## Stage 5: Superannuation allocation pathway

The longer term proposal includes a minimum allocation pathway for superannuation providers, beginning at 40 percent and potentially rising to 75 percent, for investments in companies or vehicles that meet Australian-benefit ownership criteria.

This part requires substantial legal and financial review. It must be consistent with trustee duties, member outcomes, diversification, risk management, liquidity, investment governance, and retirement income objectives.

## Design principles

Any Australian-benefit ownership mechanism should:

- protect superannuation members
- avoid forced uneconomic investment
- prevent concentration of control
- maintain competition
- avoid cartel behaviour
- comply with procurement law and trade commitments
- support long term productive capacity
- keep public value circulating in Australia

## MVP position

The MVP supports the goal of Australian-benefit ownership. It does not yet claim that a hard ownership mandate is legally ready.

The first practical step is transparency, followed by lawful procurement scoring and incentives.

The superannuation pathway does not require trustees to make uneconomic investments. Government co-investment instruments, concessional finance, and pooled vehicles can be designed so that Australian-benefit ownership positions offer competitive risk-adjusted returns. Infrastructure investment by superannuation is already substantial — the question is whether procurement policy can create investment-grade instruments in government-funded operating companies that meet both trustee duties and public-benefit objectives.
