# Risks and Objections

This document records serious objections and design risks. A credible policy must face these directly.

## Objection: This is protectionism

Response: The policy should be framed as public-benefit procurement, domestic value retention, transparency, workforce security, and long term public value. However, hard domestic ownership rules may raise procurement and trade law issues. This is why the MVP begins with transparency, procurement scoring, and incentives before mandates.

## Objection: This is socialism

Response: The proposal does not require government to own or operate the suppliers. It preserves competition between independent suppliers. The difference is that suppliers receiving public money would accept public-benefit obligations.

## Objection: This will reduce competition

Response: This is a real risk and implementation should be staged, sector specific, and monitored. The policy should include formation support, EOI pathways, and procurement onboarding so skilled workers and local operators can form new suppliers before incumbents are displaced. However, the concern about reduced competition should be weighed against the current reality: many government funded markets are already dominated by a small number of large corporate suppliers operating on panels, with high barriers to entry for smaller or newer providers. The policy is as likely to increase meaningful competition — by creating viable pathways for skilled workers and local operators to enter the market — as it is to reduce it.

## Objection: Not-for-profit suppliers can also be inefficient

Response: Correct. The proposal should not assume that every not-for-distribution structure is efficient or high quality. Public-Benefit Suppliers should be assessed on service quality, cost, transparency, workforce standards, and public-benefit outcomes.

## Objection: Public-Benefit Suppliers may use subcontracting to avoid the rules

Response: The policy should include subcontracting caps, related party transaction reporting, audit powers, and loss of status for evasion.

## Objection: Salary caps may deter skilled leaders

Response: The proposed cap is high enough to attract senior expertise while preventing extreme executive extraction from public funds. The exact threshold should be modelled and indexed.

## Objection: A ban on casual employment may reduce flexibility

Response: The default should be secure employment for ordinary ongoing work. Narrow exceptions may be needed for genuinely irregular, emergency, seasonal, student, trial, or short term work. These exceptions should be auditable.

## Objection: Superannuation funds cannot be forced to invest for political reasons

Response: This is a central legal and fiduciary constraint. Any superannuation pathway must serve member interests, meet risk and return standards, maintain diversification, and comply with trustee duties. The MVP therefore begins with incentives and investment vehicles rather than forced uneconomic investment.

## Objection: Ownership requirements may breach procurement commitments

Response: This requires legal review. The MVP separates lower risk transparency and scoring mechanisms from higher risk hard mandates.

## Objection: The policy may create compliance burden

Response: Compliance burden should be proportionate to contract size and risk. Small suppliers should receive model documents, shared services, and simplified reporting.

## Objection: The policy could create sham public-benefit entities

Response: This is a known risk. The status must include audit powers, related party rules, beneficial ownership transparency, subcontracting limits, and meaningful penalties.

## Objection: For-profit suppliers will exit the market rather than accept lower margins

Response: This objection is not well supported by the evidence of how industries respond to regulated margin compression. Norway's petroleum sector provides a clear and often-cited example: high resource taxes and strict regulatory requirements significantly reduced the share of revenue available to private operators, but did not cause industry exit. Companies continued to participate because a lower margin on a reliable, large-scale, government-backed revenue stream remained commercially attractive. The same logic applies here. Government contracts are among the most reliable, low-risk revenue streams available to any supplier. A contractor earning a reduced but still positive margin on a guaranteed multi-year contract does not walk away from that contract. They adjust their cost structures, their overhead, and their expectations. The policy does not eliminate profit. It reduces extraction and redirects a share of value toward Australian workers, Australian communities, and Australian reinvestment. Industries will still participate.

## Objection: Public-Benefit Suppliers may cost more than for-profit suppliers

Response: In some sectors and during some transition periods, this may be true, and the policy should acknowledge it honestly rather than overclaim. However, three things follow from that acknowledgement. First, cost equivalence at transition — not cost reduction — is a sufficient starting condition. A Public-Benefit Supplier that matches the cost of an incumbent while reinvesting its surplus into service quality, workforce development, and local capacity is already delivering more public value per dollar spent, even before any efficiency gains emerge. Second, the overhead structures of large corporate contractors — executive remuneration, management layers, offshore dividend flows, related party transactions, and subcontracting margins — create significant headroom for new suppliers to absorb transition costs and still remain competitive. Third, the long-term case for this policy is not primarily about immediate cost reduction. It is about what government funded markets look like in ten and twenty years when reinvestment obligations, secure employment, and Australian ownership structures have had time to compound. The transformation is structural and generational, not transactional and immediate.

## Revision: Penalties and non-compliance

Early drafts of this proposal included imprisonment for repeat serious offenders. The project has moved away from this as a standard regulatory penalty for several reasons: bespoke imprisonment provisions for regulatory breach are unusual in Australian corporate law, they create significant drafting complexity, they are likely to generate disproportionate political opposition, and existing criminal law already covers the most serious conduct — fraud, false accounting, wage theft, bribery, and deliberate evasion. The preferred model is graduated civil enforcement with referral to existing criminal pathways where appropriate.
